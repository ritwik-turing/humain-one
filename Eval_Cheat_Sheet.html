The whole picture, in plain English

For you, before the meeting. Not a script.
The settingHUMAIN ONE is a marketplace. Outside developers build AI agents, small software workers that talk to citizens and do tasks, like answering questions about permits. A developer submits their agent, a human verifier decides whether it can be listed, and approved agents go live for a government body such as a municipality. This eval product is the workbench the developer uses to find out what is wrong with their agent and fix it, both before launch and after.
What an evaluation actually isYou cannot read an agent and know if it is good, the way you cannot read a driver and know if they can drive. The only way is to test it: ask it hundreds of realistic questions and judge what comes back. An evaluation is exactly that, a big list of test questions called cases, plus machinery that scores the answers. A run is one sitting of the whole test.
What "in production" meansBefore launch, the agent only ever answers test questions. Mistakes are free, nobody real is on the other end. "In production" means the agent is live: real citizens are talking to it, and a mistake now has consequences, wrong information given, or worse, one citizen shown another citizen's details. The same agent behaves differently against real people than against a test sheet, which is why evaluation cannot stop at launch.
Why the data refreshes dailyA test set goes stale. Real people ask things nobody predicted: a new policy arrives, a season changes, people phrase things in new ways. An agent that is only tested on last month's questions will pass the test and fail on the street. So the dataset syncs nightly from the data warehouse, and the product samples one in fifty real conversations, with personal details masked, into the test set. Yesterday's real traffic becomes today's exam. And when something fails in production, that exact conversation becomes a permanent test case, so the same failure can never slip through silently again. That is the closed loop the demo keeps referring to.
Why connect a warehouse instead of uploading filesOrganisations keep their records and logs in one central place, a data warehouse like Snowflake. Connecting to it means the test set updates itself from the source of truth. Uploading CSVs by hand means someone has to remember to do it, and the test set quietly drifts away from reality.
Who is whoThe developer builds the agent, reads its failures, writes notes and checks. The platform runs everything that needs no knowledge of the agent: did it finish, did it loop, did it leak data. The judge is an AI that grades answers at scale, cheap but only trustworthy once its grades are compared against a human's, which is what the agreement score is. The verifier is the human who decides marketplace entry. The tenant is the government body whose citizens use the agent.
Who sets up the judgeThree different owners. The mechanical and safety checks are ours, shipped, not configurable by anyone. The category judges, accuracy and trustworthiness and helpfulness, are written and calibrated by the marketplace team who own the reviewed answer set, so every agent in a category is scored the same way; the developer can only switch them on or off. The developer writes their own checks on screen 5, in their own words, and the product shows how often each one agreed with them before it is trusted.
What can be judged in productionLess than before launch, and this matters. A live conversation has no expected answer, nobody wrote down what the right reply should have been, so accuracy cannot score live traffic at all. What still works is anything that reads the reply on its own: safety, language, the developer's saved checks, and grounding a claim against the citizen's record. To give a sampled production case a real accuracy score, a human has to write the expected answer first. That labelling step is the price of the closed loop, and it is worth naming rather than glossing.
Why there is no pass markA single score invites gaming: build to the test, hide the gaps. Instead the verifier receives everything, including a list of what was not checked, and makes a human decision. The gate is transparency, not a number.
Plain wordsCase: one test question with the answer you expect. Dataset: all the cases. Run: one pass of the agent over the dataset. Check: a yes-or-no question asked of every answer. Metric: a score across the whole run. Judge: an AI that grades answers. Attention set: the handful of cases picked out as worth a human look. Trace: the step-by-step record of what the agent did inside one case. Regression: something that used to work and now does not. Sampling: copying a small share of real traffic into the test set. PII masking: stripping names, numbers and identifiers before anyone sees a conversation.

Why evaluation cannot stop at launch

The argument for watching production, and where the industry landed
Short versionAn agent can get worse without anybody touching it. Testing before launch only proves it worked against the questions we thought to ask, on the day we asked them.
1. The agent stays still, the world movesThe model provider updates the model underneath you. The documents it reads get edited. A regulation changes. Nobody deployed anything and the answers are different. This is the one that matters most, because there is no code change to trigger a retest. Without production monitoring there is no moment at which anyone would think to look.
2. The numbers do not workWe test on 380 cases. A live agent might handle fifty thousand conversations a month. A failure that hits half a percent of citizens is statistically invisible in a test set, you would expect to see it once or twice and could easily see it zero times. In production that same rate is 250 people a month getting a wrong answer.
3. You cannot test for what you have not imaginedNobody writes a test case for an instruction hidden inside an uploaded document until it happens to somebody once. Every genuinely new failure mode arrives in production first. The question is only whether you find it or a journalist does.
4. The test set is a guess about what people will askReal traffic is the answer to that guess, and the two drift apart quietly. New season, new policy, new phrasing. An agent tested only on last month's questions passes the test and fails on the street.
5. Government makes this sharperRules change often and matter a lot. An agent that was compliant in March is non compliant in April with no code change at all. If compliance is only ever checked once at certification, that agent stays listed and stays wrong until somebody complains.
The honest costContinuous evaluation costs money and produces noise. If nobody acts on what it surfaces, it is theatre with a dashboard. That is exactly why what happens when a live agent gets worse is on my open list, and why I would rather agree the response before we build more of the watching.
Where the industry landedThis is not a theory we invented. Both leading platforms have built the same shape in the last year. LangSmith runs online evaluators automatically on production traces with no deployment, and uses those traces to build the datasets you then evaluate against. Arize AX added continuous production evaluation plus Signal, a managed agent that continuously reviews production traces, groups related failures, and surfaces ranked issues. That is our attention set, arrived at independently. Both also tie their judges back to human judgment rather than trusting them outright. Sources: LangSmith platform and production monitoring docs; Arize AX 2026 product notes.
Say thisAn agent can get worse without anyone touching it. The model underneath changes, the documents change, a rule changes. There is no deployment to trigger a retest, so if we are not watching production, nobody ever thinks to look. Both LangSmith and Arize built continuous production evaluation into their platforms in the last year, and Arize independently built failure clustering that looks a lot like our attention set. We are not proposing something exotic, we are proposing where the category has already gone.
If they say it is prematureReasonable for day one, and the always-on checks work from the first upload with no production data at all. The part I would not defer is the plumbing that samples live conversations, because a failure you did not capture is a failure you cannot turn into a test case, and that is the loop that stops the same problem happening twice.

WhatThe gov-services-eval dataset, 380 cases across 38 citizen journeys in Arabic and English, synced nightly from Snowflake, with connectors for CSV, warehouse, S3 or GCS, and sampled production traffic. Push back"Why can safety not be turned off?" A check the developer can disable is not a guarantee the marketplace can make. The developer most likely to leak citizen data is the one least likely to tick the box.

What the agent did / What went wrong

Screens 3 and 4 · the developer improvement loop
WhatStep-by-step trace with timing bars and payloads, a notes box in the developer's own words, and notes auto-grouped and counted with rename and merge.
WhyEvaluation criteria cannot be fixed before seeing outputs; practitioners refine them by reading failures. The product operationalises exactly that loop. Criteria drift: Shankar et al., UIST 2024. Error analysis via open coding: standard practice in applied LLM evaluation.
Say thisWe never see the developer's code and never suggest a fix. We make the failure legible and count what they tell us, which is what actually gets agents fixed.
Push back"Is a free-text note rigorous enough?" The notes become groups, groups become counts, and counts prioritise the next fix. It is the documented workflow of the strongest eval teams, not a compromise.

Turn notes into checks

Screen 5 · notes become permanent tests, and the judge earns trust
WhatA note the developer wrote becomes a saved check that runs on every case of every future run. Before anyone relies on a check, the product shows how often it agreed with the developer on cases they graded by hand: 17 of 20 here, with the three disagreements listed row by row.
WhyEvaluation criteria drift as developers read outputs, so automated judges must be validated against human grades rather than assumed correct. Showing the agreement record before a check gates anything is the published state of the art. EvalGen, Shankar et al., UIST 2024: "Who Validates the Validators?" The 2026 industry standard formalises the same loop: hand-grade a sample, compute judge-to-human agreement, accept at kappa 0.6 and above, recalibrate monthly.
Say thisMost tools let you write a judge and trust it blindly. Here the judge earns trust: it shows you exactly where it disagrees with you before it ever scores a run, and every saved check carries that record into the evidence pack.
Push back"Is 20 hand-graded cases enough?" It is the starting signal, and the screen says exactly how many grades back the number. Every case the developer reviews afterwards sharpens it. The alternative shipped by everyone else is zero validation.

What broke since last time

Screen 6 · regressions, including invisible ones
WhatTwo most recent runs compared per case, with a band for new safety flags even when no column changed.
WhyThe scariest regression is the one the columns cannot show: the demo contains a fix that introduces a prompt-injection flag while every mechanical value stays identical.
Say thisThe developer's own fix caused this flag. The screen separates "your change broke this" from "this was already broken", which is the difference between panic and a plan.
Push back"Only two runs?" The across-runs strip on this screen and the sparkline card on results cover the full history: pass rate 74 to 97 percent over five runs, accuracy up 14 points, and the safety line dips then rises when the bad fix lands. Detail stays two-run because that is the decision a developer actually makes: keep this change or revert it.

Evidence and Submit

Screens 7 and 8 · what the verifier receives
WhatAuto-assembled pack: every check listed with what happened to it, including the ones that did not run and why. Open issues declared, not hidden. Submit is never gated by a score.
WhySilence about a metric reads the same as a pass, which is how certifications get torn apart. Stating coverage explicitly is what separates an assessed tier from a self-declared one. Mirrors tiered attestation standards for agent vendors; goes beyond Microsoft Marketplace, which runs no output evaluation at all.
Say thisThere is no pass bar. Evaluation earns its place by being useful, and the verifier sees exactly what was and was not checked, so trust comes from coverage rather than a single number.
Push back"No minimum score, really?" The gate is the verifier plus total transparency of coverage. A bar invites gaming the number; a coverage statement invites fixing the agent.

The end state, end to end

Where this product goes, one loop
IngestDatasets arrive from a warehouse table (Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks), a bucket of logs (S3, GCS), a CSV, or sampled production traffic after PII masking. Mapping happens once per source.
EvaluateAlways-on mechanical and safety checks on every case, judge metrics where the category has a reviewed answer set, run strategies (bias, robustness, reliability) priced before you commit.
ImproveFailures cluster into an attention set. The developer reads traces, writes notes, notes become groups, groups become saved checks, and every check carries its agreement record before it scores anything.
CertifyRun history shows the arc, the regression view catches what a fix broke including invisible safety regressions, and the evidence pack states what was and was not checked for the verifier. No pass bar; the gate is transparency.
MonitorPublished agents feed sampled production conversations back into the dataset. A production failure becomes tomorrow morning attention case, which closes the loop: the same screens serve pre-release evaluation and live monitoring.
Say thisEvaluation is not a stage the agent passes through once. It is the loop the marketplace runs forever, and every part of that loop is on these screens today.

Answer anything

Someone asks, you look it up. Answers are short enough to paste back.
The basics
What is this thing?A workbench for developers who build agents for the marketplace. It runs their agent against a few hundred realistic citizen questions, shows them exactly what went wrong, and produces the record a verifier needs before the agent can be listed.
What is a case?One citizen question, usually with the answer we expect written down next to it. 380 of them make up the test set.
What is a run?One pass of the agent over every case in the set. Takes about six minutes for 380.
What is a check?A yes or no question asked of every answer. Did it leak a phone number. Did it reply in Arabic. Checks either fire or they don't.
Check or metric, what is the difference?A check is yes or no on one answer. A metric is a number across the whole run. Accuracy is a metric, sensitive data is a check.
What is a judge?An AI that grades answers, because a human cannot grade 380 of them every run. We only trust it as far as we have compared its grades against a human's.
What is an attention case?One of the twelve we pulled out as worth a person actually reading. They are clustered from the failures across all 380, not a random sample.
What is a trace?The step by step record of what the agent did inside one case. Which lookups it made, how long each took, where it went wrong.
What is a regression?Something that used to work and now does not, usually caused by the developer's own last change.
What does in production mean?The agent is live and real citizens are talking to it. Before that it only ever answers test questions, so mistakes are free.
Who is the verifier?The human who decides whether an agent gets listed on the marketplace. Our evidence pack is what they read.
Who is the tenant?The government body whose citizens use the agent. Al Noor Municipality in the demo.
Numbers in the demo
Why 380 cases?38 citizen journeys with about 10 questions each. Enough to be a real test set rather than a sample, and it matches the scale in the brief we were sent.
Where did the 380 come from?They are generated for the demo, seeded so the numbers never move between demos. In a real deployment they sync nightly from the tenant's warehouse.
Why only 12 attention cases?Nobody reviews 380 rows one at a time. Those twelve are clustered from the failures so a developer reads twelve and covers most of what broke. Click All cases and you get the full 380.
Why 187 Arabic?Roughly half. The dataset covers both languages because the citizens do, and the answer language check exists because an agent replying in English to an Arabic question is a real failure.
What is 30.78 SAR?Made up. It is what one run over 380 cases would cost if a case cost about five halalas. Placeholder until someone gives me the real unit cost.
Why five runs of history?So the trend lines mean something. The safety line dips over four runs then jumps back up on the fifth, which is a fix that broke something else.
What is 17 of 20?How often a saved check agreed with the developer on twenty cases they graded by hand. It is how a check earns the right to be trusted.
What does 11 of 15 ran mean?On the evidence screen. Fifteen checks exist, eleven ran this time, and the pack names the four that did not and why.
The nine metrics
AccuracyDid it say the right thing. Compared against the reviewed answer for each case. Only possible where a category has a written answer set.
TrustworthinessIs every claim in the reply actually supported by the citizen's record, rather than plausible sounding invention.
SafetyDid the reply leak personal data, contain harmful content, follow an instruction hidden in a document, or answer in the wrong language. Always on.
HelpfulnessCould a citizen act on the answer as written, or would they have to come back and ask again.
RobustnessDoes the answer hold up when you reword the question. Typos, rephrasings, the way real people actually type.
Bias and fairnessRun the same case twice changing only a name or a region, then compare. If the answer changes, that is the problem.
LatencyHow long each case took. Median across the run, plus the slowest one.
MultilingualDid it reply in the language it was asked in.
ReliabilityRun everything three times. An agent that is right once and wrong twice is not right.
Why it works this way
Why can safety not be switched off?Two cases in the demo pass every single column and still leak a phone number or follow an injected instruction. If safety were a toggle, the developer most likely to ship that agent is the one least likely to tick the box.
Why is there no pass mark?A single score invites building to the test. Instead the verifier gets everything, including a list of what was not checked, and makes a human call. The gate is transparency, not a number.
Why do the judge scores say signal, not measurement?Because nobody has checked our AI grader against a human on this dataset yet. Until that happens, treating 78 percent as a fact would be dishonest, so the screen says so itself.
Why does the evidence pack list what was not checked?Silence about a check reads exactly like a pass. That is how certifications get pulled apart later.
Why does the data refresh daily?Test sets go stale. New policies, new phrasings, things nobody predicted. An agent tested only on last month's questions passes the test and fails on the street.
Why connect Snowflake instead of uploading a file?A connected source updates itself. A CSV means somebody has to remember, and the test set quietly drifts away from what citizens are actually asking.
Why does bias cost twice as much?Because it is not a score on the run you already did. It reruns every case with one detail changed, so 380 cases become 760 runs. The price is shown before you commit.
Why does a case show green everywhere and still get flagged?Because the mechanical columns only watch the shape of the run. They cannot read the answer. Case 11 finished fast, stayed on track, returned usable text, and gave out somebody else's phone number.
Harder questions
Is this not just testing?It is testing, with two differences. The developer cannot know in advance what to test for, so the product helps them find out by reading real failures. And it never stops, because the agent behaves differently against real citizens than against a test sheet.
Is any of this real, or is it a mockup?It is a working prototype. The interactions all function, the numbers all derive from the data behind them, and nothing is a picture of a screen. What is not real is the data itself and the costs.
How is this different from Prism?Prism is the engine underneath. This is what a developer sees and does. Prism can already run a dataset against an agent and score it; what it does not have is the guided journey, the clustering, or the evidence pack.
What stops a developer gaming it?Not much, and that is deliberate. There is no score to game because there is no pass mark. What the verifier sees is coverage, open issues the developer declared, and what was not checked.
Who wrote the judges?For category metrics like accuracy, the marketplace team who own the answer set, so every agent in a category is graded the same way. Developers write their own checks separately and those show their agreement record.
What happens if the AI grader is wrong?Right now the score is labelled as a signal, so nothing automatic happens on it. That is why our regression rules never suspend an agent over a judge score, only over unambiguous things like a data leak.
Scope
What is actually built?Everything in the prototype works end to end: the dataset browser, all nine metrics with drilldowns, the trace view, notes and grouping, saved checks with agreement records, run history, the regression view, the evidence pack and its print export.
What is designed but not built?Turning a sampled production conversation into a scored case needs a human to write the expected answer, and there is no screen for that yet. Multi turn conversations are not modelled at all.
What did the client actually reject, and what changed?V1 was called dense and not exhaustive. It argued its case in paragraphs on every screen while showing twelve cases and four columns. V2 shows nine metrics, 380 cases, five runs of history and a working checks library, and the arguments moved behind small (i) buttons.
Why is there a V1 and a V2?V1 is the version that was reviewed last week. I left it untouched so the old link still shows what was actually reviewed, rather than quietly changing underneath anyone.
The open questions
How does a developer know their checks are enough?They do not. We hand them a blank box. Options are a starter set per category, or using our own failure clustering to show which clusters have nothing watching them.
How do we judge an agent once it is live?Live conversations have no expected answer, so accuracy cannot run at all. Best cheap option is signals the tenant already has, like whether the citizen asked again or gave up.
Compliance, us or Certify?Split it. Eval takes anything a computer can recheck every run, Certify takes the human call. Rules change, and if it all sits in Certify then a rule change silently invalidates everything already approved.
What about conversations rather than single questions?Not started. Everything is one question and one answer, and some failures only appear across turns.
What about a category with no answer set?The agent still runs and gets everything that needs no answer key. It just has no accuracy score, and the screen says so. Comparing two agents only means anything once both were scored on the same answer set.
Who pays?My proposal is whoever the check protects. Safety free and unlimited, category scores free within a fair budget, the expensive reruns on the developer, production monitoring on the tenant.
Who keeps the AI grader honest?Whoever owns the answer set, since they are the only ones who can produce human grades. And the product should show when each judge was last checked, so the rot is visible rather than assumed away.
Who checks our own PII stripper?Nobody yet, which is the uncomfortable one. It should be evaluated with our own product: hand tagged conversations, 99 percent on national IDs and bank details, and the sampling pipeline halts by itself if it drops.
What happens when a live agent gets worse?My proposal: a safety problem pulls it from new installs within the hour, basics slipping gives seven days then a mark on the listing, and a judge score moving does nothing at all.
